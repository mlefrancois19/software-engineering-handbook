# 10 — Hiring

Hiring is the highest-leverage decision an engineering team makes: every hire changes the team's capacity, standards, and culture for years. The bar is the one thing that is never compromised — a team that lowers it once teaches everyone that compromises happen, and a bad hire costs far more than a slow hire. And the process is a product: candidates experience it first-hand, and a badly run process repels the very people you want.

## The bar — before advertising anything

- **Write the role from a real need, not a wishlist**: define the outcome ("what will this person deliver in their first 90 days?") before the requirements. A stack checklist is how you end up with a team of one-trick specialists who cannot adapt.
- **Calibrate with the whole team**: agree what "good" means for this team and document a scoring rubric *before* the first interview. Interviewers rating on vibes is how inconsistent hiring happens.
- **Hire for trajectory, not just the present**: skills are learnable, judgment is not. A strong generalist who can learn the stack usually beats a specialist who cannot grow.
- **Bias is a process problem, not a character problem**: structured interviews, standardized questions, and evidence-based scorecards are the mitigation. Replace "culture fit" gut checks with observable values — e.g., how the candidate handles disagreement.
- **Referrals are a source, not a pass**: the same bar applies; the only advantage is faster background on the person.

## The process — interview design

- **One signal per interview**: each interviewer owns one dimension (coding, system design, communication/collaboration, technical depth). No interviewer evaluates everything — that produces overlapping noise and missing coverage.
- **Use the predictors that work**: structured interviews and work-sample tests correlate best with job performance; unstructured "let's chat about your CV" hours correlate worst. Probe real reasoning — questions asked, edge cases found, how feedback is received.
- **No hazing**: brainteasers and algorithm trivia are biased and irrelevant unless the role is literally algorithms. Live coding is a *collaboration exercise* — the candidate thinks out loud with you, not under examination.
- **Design exercises at the role's level**: a backend candidate does realistic backend work; system design is assessed at the depth the role needs. A take-home is acceptable only with a strict time cap (2–4 hours) and honest respect for the effort.
- **Score immediately and individually** at the end of each interview, against the rubric, before anyone talks — then debrief as a team and read the evidence, not the strongest personality.
- **Include the people who will work with them**: a future-teammate interview adds a different axis than the hiring manager's.

## Decisions

- **The debrief decides, not the loudest voice**: hire/no-hire comes from the collected evidence. Disagreements are settled by weighing the rubric, not by seniority.
- **"Maybe" is a no**: if the evidence does not support a hire, do not hold the candidate in limbo — indefinite holds burn goodwill and rarely convert.
- **Speed is a feature**: good candidates have competing offers; run the process in under ~2 weeks total, schedule pre-communicated, every interview confirmed.
- **Offer fairly and once**: pre-communicated salary bands; do not nickel-and-dime the final offer — the cost of losing (or re-hiring for) a good candidate dwarfs the difference.
- **Check references for substance**: ask for specifics ("what changed because of them? how did they respond to failure?"), not boilerplate.
- **Reject kindly and finally**: prompt, personal, clear. Rejected candidates are future applicants, customers, and references — and they talk.

## Onboarding — the second half of hiring

- **Day one means day one**: access, credentials, and a first small task on day one; a week of "you'll get access soon" teaches everyone the org is broken.
- **A structured ramp**: 30/60/90 goals agreed on day one — a buddy, pairing for the first weeks, and an early visible win. Checkpoints are for feedback, not surveillance.
- **Match the ramp to the role**: time-to-productivity is a tracked metric, and the expectation is explicit ("solid by month 2, autonomous by month 3" for a mid-level hire).
- **The hiring rubric becomes the growth rubric**: the dimensions scored at hiring become the calibration language for 1:1s and reviews — one standard from candidate to senior.

## What good hiring never does

- **Compromise the bar under pressure**: "we really need headcount" produces the hires you will spend a year unwinding. Hire slowly in doubt, fire fast in fact.
- **Hire the brilliant jerk**: a high-output hire who damages the team's culture costs more than their output — standards and velocity are collective goods.
- **Over-specify the stack**: "10 years in <framework>" filters out great generalists and is usually a fantasy requirement anyway; specify the problem, not the version history.
- **Run a gauntlet**: five-plus rounds each re-exploring the same ground signals disorganization and wastes everyone's time.
- **Confuse culture fit with culture add**: you do not need people like the people you already have; you need people who extend the team.

## Metrics worth tracking

| Metric | What it tells you |
|--------|-------------------|
| Time-to-hire (req → accepted offer) | Process efficiency; target under ~2 weeks of interviewing |
| Offer acceptance rate | Market competitiveness of the offer + process quality |
| Candidate experience (survey/NPS) | Reputation; a bad score repels candidates for years |
| Funnel diversity by stage | Where underrepresented candidates drop off — and what that says about the process |
| Time-to-productivity | Ramp health; onboarding quality |
| Retention at 1 year | Whether the bar actually predicted fit |
| Source quality | Where the best hires come from — invest there |
